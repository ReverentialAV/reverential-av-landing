Why Your Wireless Microphone Keeps Dropping Out

A wireless microphone dropout at the wrong moment — the pastor mid-sentence, the soloist on the last note — is one of the most disruptive failures in a live service. It is also almost entirely preventable.

Thomas Jeffrin Founder, Reverential AV Integration 18 October 2026 6 min read

I have encountered wireless dropout in every category of venue, from small house churches to large conference auditoriums. In almost every case, the cause was not a faulty receiver or a failing transmitter. It was a frequency coordination problem — multiple wireless systems occupying frequencies that interfere with each other, or occupying frequencies no longer legally available in that region.

Dropout occurs when the receiver cannot distinguish the transmitter's signal from other radio frequency activity in the same space.

The five most common causes

1. No frequency coordination

Every wireless system shipped from the factory is set to a default frequency. If a facility has multiple wireless systems and none have been coordinated, some will occupy overlapping intermodulation frequencies that create interference between units. This happens even when each system appears to be on a different frequency.

Frequency coordination is a calculation: given the frequencies in use, what are the intermodulation products, and are they landing on any active frequencies? Professional coordination software (Shure Wireless Workbench, Sennheiser Wireless Systems Manager) does this automatically. It takes approximately one hour per system. Almost no budget installation does it.

2. Frequencies outside the legal window

Wireless microphone systems in India must operate within frequency ranges licensed by TRAI. These ranges have changed as spectrum has been reallocated to mobile data services. Equipment purchased several years ago may be operating on frequencies now used by 4G LTE networks — and losing the battle.

3. Antenna placement

RF signals are line-of-sight. The receiver antenna must have a clear sightline to the transmitter. Antennas hidden behind metal equipment racks or placed at floor level in a space where the transmitter moves at head height are fighting the physics. Remote antenna placement — a coaxial cable from the rack to an antenna in the optimal room position — solves this in most spaces.

4. Transmitter battery failure

Lithium batteries in wireless transmitters do not discharge linearly. They maintain voltage at nearly full capacity until approximately 80–85% of their life, then drop suddenly. Policy: replace all transmitter batteries before every service. For high-traffic installations, rechargeable systems eliminate this failure mode entirely.

Frequency coordination is not a luxury for large venues. It is the minimum requirement for any space running more than two wireless systems.

5. RF interference from LED fixtures

Modern LED stage lighting — particularly low-quality units without proper RF shielding — emits broadband interference in the UHF spectrum. If wireless dropout began after a lighting upgrade, this is the likely cause. The fix is RF-shielded LED fixtures or a review of wireless frequencies to find clear channels above the noise floor the LED units are generating.

The systematic fix

An RF scan of the space with a spectrum analyser identifies occupied frequencies and noise floor before any channel assignment. Coordination software then assigns clean channels to each system. Antenna placement is reviewed and corrected. The result is a wireless system that operates reliably in its specific RF environment, not in the generic environment it was factory-tested in.

This process takes half a day for a small installation. It rarely costs more than the service call to investigate a dropout problem that has been ongoing for months.
