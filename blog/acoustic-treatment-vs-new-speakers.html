Acoustic Treatment or New Speakers: How to Know Which One You Actually Need

Most churches replace their sound system two or three times before anyone measures the room. Each replacement inherits exactly the same problem as the equipment it replaced. Here is why that cycle keeps repeating, and how to break it.

Thomas Jeffrin Founder, Reverential AV Integration 11 October 2026 5 min read

I do not start system design conversations with equipment. I start with a question: has this room been measured? If the answer is no, then whatever we buy next will be specified for a room we do not understand, solving a problem we have not diagnosed.

Sound quality problems in a room come from three places: the room itself (acoustics), the system design (how the equipment is configured), and the operation (how it is run). Most systems that sound bad are suffering from all three, in that order of severity. Treating the third without addressing the first two produces a well-run bad-sounding system.

How to tell if your problem is the room

The clap test. Stand in the centre of your empty space. Clap once, sharply. Listen. If you hear a distinct tail of sound lasting more than 1.5 seconds, your room is reverberant. New speakers will not reduce it.

The speech test. Have someone speak at the lectern with the system off. Walk to the back. If speech is completely smeared by the room even without amplification, the room needs treatment before any system can fix it.

The vocal test. Run the system at normal level, without music. Walk from front to back. If clarity drops dramatically mid-room, you have a system design problem. If clarity is equally poor across all rows, you have an acoustic problem.

An untreated room does not become better with better speakers. It becomes better with treatment, then better again with better speakers.

When treatment comes first

If the clap test reveals a reverb tail beyond 1.5–2.0 seconds in a speech-primary space, treatment should precede any equipment decision. The equipment needs to be designed for the treated room, not the untreated one.

A system designed for 2.4-second RT60 is a different system from one designed for 0.9-second RT60. The coverage angles, delay times, and DSP alignment all change when the room changes. Specifying equipment for the untreated room means re-specifying it after treatment.

When new speakers come first

If room acoustics are already within range (below 1.2 seconds RT60 at 500 Hz) and clarity problems persist, the system design is the likely cause. Common failures in existing installations: incorrect coverage angle; no delay fills for the back rows; no time alignment between speaker clusters; incorrect gain structure; no DSP alignment.

These are system design problems. In most cases, they can be corrected through proper DSP configuration and alignment without replacing any equipment. Before specifying new speakers, have the existing system measured and aligned. You may find the problem was never the speakers.

The sequence

Measure the room. Treat the room where needed. Design a system for the treated room. Commission the system with measurement. Document the result. In every project we take on, this sequence is non-negotiable. It is slower than arriving with a truck of equipment. It produces better outcomes and avoids the next replacement cycle.
