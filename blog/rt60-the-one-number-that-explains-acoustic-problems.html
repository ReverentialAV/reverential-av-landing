RT60: The One Number That Explains Every Acoustic Problem in Your Space

You do not need a measurement microphone to understand RT60. You need a room, a hand clap, and about thirty seconds. What you hear after the clap tells you everything about why your sound system is struggling.

RT60 stands for Reverberation Time 60 — the number of seconds it takes for sound in a room to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops. In plain terms: if you clap once in an empty church, RT60 is how long the clap keeps ringing.

You can measure this with a calibrated microphone and measurement software, which gives you a precise number at each frequency band. You can also estimate it with your ears: stand in your empty space, clap sharply, and count the seconds until you no longer hear any trace of the clap. That rough count is your RT60.

Why RT60 controls clarity

Speech works by sequence. One syllable follows another. The listener's brain stitches them together into words. When a room has a high RT60, each syllable is still ringing by the time the next one arrives. The listener hears not one syllable but two: the new one arriving and the old one still fading. This is the acoustic basis of the most common complaint in worship spaces: "we can hear the sound but we cannot understand the words."

This is not a loudspeaker problem. Turning up the volume makes it worse, not better. A louder signal produces a louder reflection. The ratio between the two — direct to reflected — does not change.

Turning up the volume does not improve clarity in a reverberant room. It increases both signals equally. The blur stays.

What the numbers mean

Target RT60 values vary by room type and primary use:

  • Speech-primary spaces (lecture theatres, corporate boardrooms): 0.6–0.9 seconds
  • Multipurpose worship spaces (contemporary church with amplified music and spoken word): 0.9–1.3 seconds
  • Music-primary spaces (pipe organ, choral, classical): 1.8–2.5 seconds
  • Typical untreated stone church in India: 2.0–3.5 seconds
  • Typical untreated concrete auditorium: 1.5–2.5 seconds

When a contemporary worship church has an RT60 of 2.4 seconds, it is operating in a space designed acoustically for classical sacred music. The reverb that made the organ sound transcendent makes the pastor's sermon unintelligible. Both are using the same room. Neither is wrong. The room is designed for one and asked to do both.

The diagnostic question

Before any AV project begins, ask your contractor: what is the RT60 of this room at 500 Hz, and what will your system design achieve? If they cannot answer, they have not measured it.

RT60 is frequency-dependent

Rooms do not have one RT60. They have a different RT60 at every frequency. Low frequencies almost always ring longer than high frequencies in untreated rooms. This is why a room can feel "boomy" — the bass is sustaining long after the mids and highs have faded.

A professional measurement produces an RT60 curve across the full frequency spectrum. A room with 0.9 seconds at 1 kHz but 3.0 seconds at 125 Hz has a bass problem that no amount of EQ on the mixing console will solve.

RT60 and STI

RT60 describes the room. STI — Speech Transmission Index — describes the outcome. STI is a single number between 0 and 1 that measures speech intelligibility at a specific seat. An STI of 0.75 or above is considered good. Below 0.45 is unintelligible. RT60 tells you the cause. STI tells you the consequence.

What to do if your RT60 is too high

The fix is acoustic treatment — absorption panels placed at the points of first reflection. This is not soundproofing. Acoustic treatment controls what sound does once it is inside the room.

Placement matters more than quantity. A room with absorption at the right first-reflection points — typically the side walls at one-third and two-thirds of the room length, and a cloud above the congregation — can reduce RT60 from 2.4 seconds to under 1.0 second without making the space feel uncomfortable.

This acoustic work comes before any loudspeaker decisions. The system is designed for the treated room, not the untreated one. Specifying speakers before treating the room is specifying for a problem that will no longer exist — and potentially creating a new one.
